    Module overview

    Why this module matters

    Older adults commonly live with several chronic conditions at once. These conditions — and aging itself — affect how they see, hear, feel, think and communicate, and they raise difficult legal and ethical questions about benefits, medications, decision-making and the end of life. The midterm module prepares you to recognize these problems early, intervene with sound rationales, and protect the older person's rights and dignity.

    Prerequisite knowledge

    • Normal anatomy and physiology of the eye, ear, nervous system and speech mechanism.
    • The nursing process (assessment → diagnosis → planning → implementation → evaluation).
    • Therapeutic communication and basic pharmacology.
    • Normal physiologic changes of aging (covered before the midterm).

    Expected competencies

    • Assess sensory, cognitive and communication deficits and their effects on safety and quality of life.
    • Plan person-centered, safe interventions with rationales and family teaching.
    • Apply evidence-based tools and principles (CGA, fall and pain assessment, medication review).
    • Uphold the rights, benefits and ethical protections of Filipino older persons.

    Disturbance in Sensory Perception

    Age-related decline in vision, hearing, touch, smell and taste — what causes it, how it looks, and how the nurse keeps the older adult safe, connected and independent.

    Definition & causes of sensory decline PDF p. 2

    Geriatric disturbance in sensory perception is the decline in sensory functions — vision, hearing, taste, smell and touch — commonly experienced by older adults. These changes significantly affect quality of life, independence and safety, and increase the risk for injury and isolation.

    Sensory decline is largely a natural part of aging, but chronic disease and lifestyle factors can accelerate or worsen it. Think of the senses as the older adult's “safety radar”: when the radar weakens, hazards (a step, a hot faucet, smoke, spoiled food) go undetected and social contact becomes harder.

    CauseWhat happensExamples from the module
    1. Aging processCells and organs responsible for sensory perception degenerate or function less efficiently.Presbyopia, presbycusis, reduced taste and smell
    2. Chronic conditionsDisease damages sensory structures, particularly vision and hearing.Diabetes (diabetic retinopathy), hypertension, cardiovascular disease
    3. MedicationsSide effects impair senses such as hearing or taste.Ototoxic drugs, chemotherapy
    4. Environmental factorsLong-term exposure hastens decline.Loud noise, harmful chemicals
    Clinical pearlA new or sudden sensory change is not “just aging.” Ask about new medications (e.g., ototoxic drugs) and control of chronic illnesses such as diabetes — these are causes the nurse can act on.

    Nursing considerations in managing sensory impairment PDF pp. 2–3

    ConsiderationWhat the nurse doesRationale
    1. Holistic, person-centered careAssess how the impairment affects overall well-being and daily life; learn the patient's preferences, needs and coping mechanisms.Care is effective only when individualized to the person's real-life limitations.
    2a. Psychosocial — social isolationHelp maintain social connections using assistive devices and supportive communication techniques.Communication difficulty leads to withdrawal and loneliness.
    2b. Psychosocial — emotional healthProvide emotional support; refer to mental health professionals when necessary.Sensory decline lowers self-esteem and confidence and can lead to depression or anxiety.
    3.     4. Promoting independenceTeach use of sensory aids (glasses, hearing aids, mobility devices); encourage participation in daily activities with adjustments. Refer to occupational therapy for adaptive techniques.Independence preserves function, dignity and quality of life.

    1. Visual disturbances PDF p. 3

    ConditionWhat it isVision affected / hallmark
    PresbyopiaAge-related difficulty focusing on nearby objects.Near vision → commonly needs reading glasses
    CataractsClouding of the eye lens.Blurred vision, glare, faded colors
    GlaucomaIncreased intraocular pressure (IOP) damaging the optic nerve.Peripheral vision loss
    Age-related macular degeneration (AMD)Degeneration of the macula.Central vision loss — reading, recognizing faces difficult
    Diabetic retinopathyComplication of diabetes; damage to retinal blood vessels.Floaters, blurred vision, or blindness
    Memory aid

    Glaucoma = Gone at the sides (peripheral, “tunnel”). Macular degeneration = Middle missing (central). Cataract = Cloudy lens, glare, colors fade. Presby-opia = “old eye” → can't read near print.

    Effects

    1. Difficulty reading, driving, or recognizing faces.
    2. Increased risk of falls and accidents due to poor depth perception.
    3. Social withdrawal due to difficulty engaging in activities.

    Nursing interventions

    1. Ensure regular eye exams — routine check-ups detect early problems; teach patients and caregivers the importance of medication adherence for eye conditions (e.g., eye drops). Why: glaucoma damage is preventable but irreversible once lost.
    2. Modify the environment — clutter-free spaces, clear pathways, contrasting colors to differentiate objects and steps; enhance lighting and reduce glare. Why: compensates for poor depth perception and glare sensitivity.
    3. Assistive devices — glasses, magnifying lenses, large-print materials, other visual aids.
    4. Fall prevention — teach fall risks; install grab bars and non-slip rugs; partner with occupational therapy for canes or other mobility aids.
    5. Summary: ensure adequate lighting, remove hazards, use contrasting colors, encourage regular eye exams.
    Exam trap“Peripheral” vs “central.” Glaucoma steals the sides; AMD steals the center. A patient who bumps into door frames but can read → think glaucoma. A patient who cannot read or see faces but walks around obstacles → think AMD.

    2. Auditory disturbances PDF p. 4

    ConditionDescription
    PresbycusisAge-related, gradual hearing loss, especially of high-pitched sounds.
    TinnitusRinging or buzzing in the ears; can accompany hearing loss.
    Conductive hearing lossBlockage in the outer or middle ear, such as earwax buildup — can be treated.
    OtosclerosisAbnormal bone growth in the middle ear → fixation of the stapes, reducing sound transmission → progressive hearing loss. The sense of balance may also be impaired.

    Effects

    1. Difficulty understanding speech, especially in noisy environments.
    2. Social isolation due to difficulty following conversations.
    3. Misinterpretation of sounds → frustration or confusion.

    Nursing interventions

    1. Hearing assessments — regular screening; audiometric screening; refer to audiologists for hearing aids or other devices.
    2. Communication techniques — clear, slow speech; simple sentences; moderate pace; without shouting; maintain eye contact; face the patient; reduce background noise.
    3. Assistive devices — encourage hearing aids or assistive listening devices and ensure they function correctly.
    4. Education — teach hearing-aid care and consistent use; teach family to speak slowly, face the patient and use visual cues.
    5. Summary: speak clearly, reduce background noise, ensure hearing aids work, and assess for cerumen impaction.
    Deeper dive: why “don't shout”?

    Shouting distorts speech sounds and tends to raise the pitch of the voice — but presbycusis affects high frequencies first, so shouting can make speech harder to understand and may look angry. Facing the patient allows lip-reading and use of facial cues. Checking for cerumen matters because conductive loss from earwax is a reversible cause that may be layered on top of presbycusis. Explanation

    3. Tactile disturbances PDF pp. 4–5

    Touch, pain, temperature and proprioception — the body's sense of its own position, movement and force in space (push/pull).

    ConditionDescription
    Peripheral neuropathyNerve damage (often due to diabetes; lack of blood flow) → numbness, tingling or burning, particularly in the hands and feet.
    Reduced tactile sensitivityAging decreases sensitivity to pain, pressure, temperature and vibration.
    Impaired proprioceptionDifficulty perceiving body position → increased fall risk.

    Effects

    1. Increased risk of burns, frostbite or injuries from diminished pain or temperature sensitivity.
    2. Difficulty with fine-motor tasks (buttoning clothes, holding utensils).
    3. Poor skin integrity → pressure ulcers if immobile.
    4. Increased risk of falls.

    Nursing interventions

    1. Assess — monitor for reduced sensation in extremities (peripheral neuropathy, diabetes).
    2. Regular skin checks — daily skin inspection to prevent pressure ulcers or injuries.
    3. Fall prevention — educate on proper footwear.
    4. Assist with mobility — tools for fine-motor tasks; ensure safe ambulation.
    5. Adjust temperature settings — warn about burns; set water heaters to safe temperatures.
    6. Sensory stimulation — touch-based activities such as massage or textured objects.
    7. Summary: caution with temperature extremes, assess for skin injury, encourage assistive devices, implement fall prevention.
    Supplementary — safe hot-water temperatureA water-heater setting of about 49 °C (120 °F) or lower is commonly recommended to reduce scald risk (U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission). Teach patients to test water with an elbow or thermometer rather than with numb hands or feet. Local recommendations may differ.

    4. Olfactory (smell) & gustatory (taste) disturbances PDF p. 5

    TermMeaning
    Age-related lossBoth taste and smell decline with age, reducing enjoyment of food.
    AnosmiaComplete loss of smell (aging or certain medications).
    HyposmiaReduced ability to smell; affects flavor perception.
    HypogeusiaReduced taste sensitivity, especially for sweet and salty flavors.
    AgeusiaLoss of taste sensation (medication, infection, or zinc deficiency).
    Memory aid-osmia = smell (think “osmosis of odors”), -geusia = taste (“gustatory”). A-/an- = none; hypo- = less.

    Effects

    1. Decreased appetite and poor nutrition.
    2. Inability to detect spoiled food or smoke → safety risk.
    3. Potential weight loss or malnutrition.

    Nursing interventions

    1. Assess — screen for changes in taste or smell affecting appetite and nutrition.
    2. Promote balanced nutrition — flavorful, well-seasoned food; herbs and spices without added salt.
    3. Smell stimulation — proper oral hygiene and sinus health; aromatic herbs and spices at meals.
    4. Safety education — warn about failing to detect spoiled food or smoke from a fire.
    5. Summary — SMELL: seasoning (not salt), check food freshness, teach safety (gas leaks, smoke detection). TASTE: good oral hygiene, hydration, appealing food presentation; monitor nutrition and appetite.
    Exam trapBecause hypogeusia blunts salty taste, older adults tend to add salt — risky with hypertension or heart failure. The correct answer is almost always “herbs and spices,” never “add more salt.”

    Summary & key takeaways

    SenseKey conditionsBiggest dangerPriority nursing focus
    VisionPresbyopia, cataract, glaucoma, AMD, diabetic retinopathyFalls (depth perception)Lighting, no glare, contrast, clear paths, eye exams, eye-drop adherence
    HearingPresbycusis, tinnitus, conductive loss, otosclerosisIsolation, misunderstandingFace patient, speak clearly, no shouting, reduce noise, working hearing aid, check cerumen
    TouchPeripheral neuropathy, reduced sensitivity, impaired proprioceptionBurns, pressure ulcers, fallsDaily skin checks, safe water temp, proper footwear, mobility aids
    Smell / tasteAnosmia, hyposmia, hypogeusia, ageusiaGas/smoke/spoiled food; malnutritionSmoke/gas safety, food freshness, herbs not salt, oral hygiene, monitor weight
    Key takeaways
    • Sensory decline threatens safety, independence and social connection — interventions target all three.
    • Always pair environmental modification with assistive devices and family teaching.
    • Refer appropriately: audiologist (hearing), eye specialist (vision), occupational therapy (adaptive techniques), mental health professionals (depression/anxiety).

    Unit 3 · Topic 2 · Module pp. 6–9

    Chronic Confusion

    Dementia in older adults: types, stages, assessment, and the nursing strategies that keep the person safe, oriented, communicating and dignified — while supporting the caregiver.

    Definition & key characteristics PDF p. 6

    Geriatric chronic confusion is a significant challenge in geriatric nursing. It results from dementia, Alzheimer's disease and other neurodegenerative disorders, and involves a progressive decline in memory, attention, orientation and judgment that makes daily activities and personal safety difficult.

    Chronic confusion — also known as dementia — is a gradual decline in cognitive function affecting memory, reasoning, language and behavior. It is distinct from delirium, an acute, reversible confusion caused by infections, medications or metabolic imbalances.

    Key characteristics

    • Persistent disorientation to time, place, and sometimes people.
    • Impaired memory, particularly short-term memory.
    • Difficulty with problem-solving and decision-making.
    • Behavioral changes — agitation, apathy, aggression.
    • Loss of ability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs).
    Supplementary — the “3 Ds” comparison
    DeliriumDementia (chronic confusion)Depression (“pseudodementia”)
    OnsetSudden (hours–days)Gradual (months–years)Relatively recent, often after loss or stressor
    CourseFluctuates; often worse at nightSlow, progressivePersistent low mood
    AttentionMarkedly impairedUsually intact earlyPoor effort/concentration
    Reversible?Usually, when the cause is treatedIrreversible, progressiveOften, with treatment
    Nursing priorityFind and treat the cause — a medical emergencySafety, routine, supportScreen mood; refer for treatment

    A person with dementia can develop delirium on top of it — any sudden worsening must be reported, not assumed to be “progression.”

    Common causes of chronic confusion PDF p. 6

    Chronic confusion is commonly linked to neurodegenerative disorders, but other factors contribute to or worsen it.

    TypeMechanism / descriptionDistinguishing features
    Alzheimer's diseaseMost common cause of dementiaGradual loss of memory, cognitive function and physical ability
    Vascular dementiaReduced blood flow to the brain from stroke or chronic cardiovascular conditionsConfusion and memory problems linked to vascular events
    Lewy body dementiaAbnormal protein deposits (Lewy bodies) in the brainCognitive fluctuations, visual hallucinations, movement disorders
    Parkinson's disease dementiaOccurs in the later stages of Parkinson's diseaseAccompanied by physical impairment and motor dysfunction
    Frontotemporal dementiaAffects the frontal and temporal lobesPersonality changes, inappropriate behavior, language difficulties

    Other contributing factors

    • Chronic illnesses — diabetes, heart disease, thyroid dysfunction can exacerbate confusion.
    • Medications — e.g., anticholinergics, sedatives can cause or worsen cognitive impairment.
    • Chronic alcohol abuse — long-term use causes cognitive deficits.
    • Depression — called “pseudodementia” when symptoms mimic dementia; often reversible with treatment.
    Memory aidLewy body = “Looks” (visual hallucinations) + “Like Parkinson” (movement) + “Live-wire” fluctuations. Frontotemporal = the “front” of the personality changes (behavior and language).

    Clinical manifestations by stage PDF p. 7

    Symptoms vary with the stage and type of dementia; recognizing them early supports early detection and management.

    Early stageMid stageLate stage
    • Forgetfulness, especially recent events or names
    • Difficulty planning or solving problems
    • Confusion with time and place
    • Difficulty following conversations or instructions
    • Increased memory loss and confusion
    • Difficulty recognizing friends and family
    • Repetitive speech or actions
    • Wandering or getting lost
    • Behavior changes — irritability, aggression, withdrawal
    • Severe impairment requiring full-time care
    • Inability to communicate verbally
    • Loss of mobility, swallowing difficulties, incontinence
    • Complete dependence for personal care
    Clinical pearlLate-stage swallowing difficulty raises the risk of aspiration and malnutrition — reasons the module's holistic care stresses nutrition and hydration, and why end-of-life planning should begin early.

    A. Assessment & early identification PDF p. 7

    Early diagnosis is key. Regular cognitive assessments may include:

    • Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) or Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) — cognitive screening tools for memory, attention and orientation.
    • Behavioral observation — changes in behavior, personality and social interaction.
    • Functional assessments — ability to perform ADLs and IADLs (instrumental ADLs: cooking, cleaning, managing finances).
    Supplementary — screening tool facts
    • MMSE (Folstein, 1975): scored out of 30; lower scores suggest cognitive impairment (a cut-off around 23–24 is often used; results are affected by education and language).
    • MoCA (Nasreddine, 2005): scored out of 30; 26 or above is generally considered normal; more sensitive than the MMSE for mild impairment.
    • Both are screening tools — they do not diagnose dementia by themselves.

    B–D. Orientation, communication & behavioral symptoms PDF pp. 7–8

    B. Promoting orientation & cognitive stimulation

    • Reorientation techniques — remind of day, time, location using large clocks, calendars, cues; a structured environment.
    • Cognitive stimulation — puzzles, reading, card games, folding laundry, music therapy.
    • Familiar objects — photos and familiar items reduce confusion and give security.
    • Structured routine — consistent schedules for meals, activities and bedtime reduce anxiety and reinforce memory.
    • Memory aids — photo albums, familiar music, objects from the past to stimulate reminiscence.

    C. Communication strategies

    • Simple, clear instructions — short sentences, one instruction at a time.
    • Non-verbal cues — touch, gestures, facial expressions.
    • Allow time for responses — do not rush.

    D. Managing behavioral symptoms (agitation, aggression, wandering)

    • Calm environment — quiet surroundings reduce agitation.
    • Redirection — gently redirect to another activity or location.
    • Consistency in caregivers — familiar faces reduce anxiety.
    • Therapeutic techniques — reality orientation or validation therapy depending on the stage.
    Reality orientationValidation therapy
    What it isGently reminding the patient of their name, the date, etc.Acknowledging the patient's emotions rather than correcting their confusion
    Best stageEarlier stagesMore advanced stages
    Example“Good morning, Mr. Cruz. Today is Monday; you are in the ward.”“You miss your mother. Tell me about her.”
    Exam trapRepeatedly correcting a person with advanced dementia (“Your mother died 30 years ago”) causes fresh grief and agitation. In advanced stages, choose the answer that acknowledges the feeling and redirects.

    E–G. Safety, caregiver support & advance care planning PDF pp. 8–9

    E. Ensuring patient safety

    • Safe environment — grab bars, non-slip mats, proper lighting; minimize loose rugs, poor lighting, sharp objects.
    • Wander prevention — bed alarms, locked doors, alert systems.
    • Medication management — review drugs that contribute to confusion; avoid polypharmacy.

    F. Support for caregivers

    • Caregiver training — progression of dementia; handling agitation or hallucinations.
    • Respite care — a temporary substitute caregiver so the family caregiver can rest, preventing burnout.
    • Support groups — sharing experiences and receiving emotional support.

    G. Advance care planning

    • Advance directives — complete them while the patient can still make decisions.
    • End-of-life care — discuss palliative and hospice care as needs change.
    Clinical pearlTiming matters: in progressive dementia, decision-making capacity is lost over time, so advance-directive discussions belong in the early stage.

    Holistic approach & conclusion PDF p. 9

    • Physical health — manage comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension); ensure nutrition, hydration and mobility to prevent infection and pressure ulcers.
    • Emotional support — reassure patient and family; confusion brings frustration, fear and loneliness.
    • Spiritual care — facilitate services that align with their beliefs.
    Key takeaways
    • Dementia = gradual, irreversible; delirium = acute, reversible; depression can mimic dementia (pseudodementia).
    • Nursing care: individualized plans, appropriate communication, safety, and support for both patient and caregiver.
    • Early identification, ongoing assessment and a multidisciplinary approach are crucial.

    Impaired Verbal Communication

    When older adults cannot express themselves or understand spoken language — the causes, the signs, and the nursing interventions that maximize remaining abilities.

    Definition PDF p. 9

    Geriatric impaired verbal communication refers to difficulties older adults experience in expressing themselves verbally or understanding spoken language. Causes include age-related changes, neurological disorders and sensory deficits.

    Because communication is fundamental to daily interactions and well-being, impairment can significantly reduce quality of life and lead to social isolation, frustration or emotional distress.

    Causes PDF pp. 9–10

    A. Age-related changes (prefix presby- = old age; senescence)

    • Presbyphonia — natural aging of the vocal cords → weak, hoarse or breathy voice.
    • Speech rate — slower speech, longer pauses, reduced articulation clarity from changes in muscle strength and coordination.

    B. Neurological disorders (CNS, cranial nerves)

    Aphasia — loss of ability to understand or express speech, commonly from stroke or other brain injury:

    TypeBrain areaComprehensionSpeech output
    Expressive (Broca's)Broca's area — frontal lobe, usually left hemisphereUnderstands speechDifficulty forming words or sentences — effortful, telegraphic (module example: “mangan siak,” Ilocano for “I eat”)
    Receptive (Wernicke's)Wernicke's area — temporal lobe, usually left hemisphereLittle understanding of languageFluent but nonsensical
    GlobalExtensive damageImpairedImpaired — both expressive and receptive affected (most severe)
    Memory aidBroca = Broken speech, Frontal, knows what he wants to say. Wernicke = Word salad, Temporal (near the ear — the “listening” area), doesn't understand.
    • Dementia — gradual, irreversible decline in memory, reasoning, language and behavior; Alzheimer's and other neurodegenerative diseases impair word-finding, following conversations and responding appropriately.
    • Parkinson's disease — affects motor function including speech; may cause dysarthria (slurred or slow speech from weakened muscle control).
    • Stroke — damages speech/language areas → difficulty speaking, understanding, reading or writing.

    C. Hearing impairment (sensory deficit)

    • Presbycusis — age-related hearing loss makes it hard to hear others, especially in noise → misunderstandings or inappropriate responses.

    D. Physical & structural issues

    • Dysarthria — speech disorder from muscle weakness or poor coordination of speech muscles (stroke, ALS, Parkinson's).
    • Dental problems — missing teeth, poorly fitting dentures, oral health issues impair articulation.
    • Respiratory issues — COPD or heart failure → breathlessness, difficulty speaking in complete sentences.
    Exam trap — aphasia vs dysarthriaAphasia is a language problem (the brain's word system). Dysarthria is a motor speech problem (the muscles). A patient with pure dysarthria understands and chooses the right words but pronounces them poorly.

    Clinical manifestations & impact PDF pp. 10–11

    Manifestations

    • Difficulty finding the right words; using incorrect words
    • Slurred, slow or incoherent speech
    • Frequent repetition of words or phrases
    • Pauses to think of words or complete sentences
    • Difficulty understanding others, especially in noise
    • Shortened or fragmented sentences
    • Gestures or body language in place of speech

    Impact

    • Social isolation — avoiding situations from embarrassment → loneliness
    • Frustration & emotional distress — anxiety, depression, anger
    • Reduced independence — trouble managing activities, following medical instructions, expressing needs
    • Safety concerns — unmet needs (hunger, pain, discomfort); accidents; miscommunication in healthcare

    Nursing interventions PDF pp. 11–12

    A. Assessment of communication abilities

    • Comprehensive speech and language assessment — standardized tools such as the MMSE or speech-language pathology assessments.
    • Observation — ability to understand and respond to verbal and nonverbal communication (look for all the clinical manifestations above).
    • Hearing and vision tests — sensory impairments worsen communication difficulties.

    B. Facilitating effective communication

    Clear, simple languageSlow, clear, moderate pace; avoid complex sentences and jargon.
    Nonverbal communicationGestures, facial expressions, visual aids (pictures, writing boards).
    Communication aidsPicture boards, flashcards, writing tools, electronic devices.
    Active listeningGive time to respond; don't rush or interrupt; be patient and empathetic.
    Rephrase and repeatUse simpler language if not understood.
    Face the patientEye contact; lips visible for lip-reading.
    ValidationValidate emotions when communication is difficult.
    Time and patienceInterrupting or rushing causes frustration and withdrawal.

    C. Speech therapy — referral to a speech-language pathologist (SLP)

    • Speech exercises — strengthen speech muscles; improve articulation and word-finding.
    • Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) — communication boards, picture cards, speech-generating devices for those who cannot communicate verbally.
    • Rehabilitation for aphasia — compensatory strategies such as gestures or writing.

    D. Environmental modifications

    • Reduce background noise.
    • Assistive devices — hearing aids, amplifiers, communication devices.
    • Adjust methods — written instructions, picture boards, text-to-speech apps.

    E. Patient & family education

    • Teach communication strategies to family and caregivers (patience, supportive techniques).
    • Encourage social interaction — family gatherings, group activities, senior community programs.
    • Safety precautions — teach caregivers to monitor for signs of distress or unmet needs, because the patient may not be able to voice discomfort.

    F. Emotional & psychological needs

    • Emotional support — reassurance; self-expression through art or music therapy.
    • Support groups — connect with others with similar difficulties.
    Clinical pearl — expressive aphasia at the bedsideAsk questions that can be answered with yes/no, a nod, or by pointing to a picture board; wait; avoid finishing the patient's sentences unless invited. Remember that the patient understands you — never talk about them as if they cannot hear or understand. Explanation

    Holistic approach & conclusion PDF pp. 12–13

    Person-centered care tailors interventions to the individual's needs, preferences and abilities, maintaining dignity, respect and empathy.

    • Physical health — monitor for infections, medication side effects or nutritional deficiencies that worsen communication.
    • Mental health — evaluate for depression or anxiety.
    • Spiritual care — spiritual or emotional counseling for those who feel disconnected.
    Key takeaways
    • Maximize remaining communication abilities, support emotional well-being and ensure safety.
    • Early assessment, ongoing SLP support and person-centered care improve outcomes.
    • Always rule out and correct hearing, vision and dental contributors.

    Core Elements of Evidence-Based Gerontological Nursing Practice

    The ten principles that ground high-quality care of older adults in the best available evidence — tailored to their physiological, psychological and social needs.

    Definition & the ten elements at a glance PDF p. 13

    Core elements of evidence-based gerontological nursing practice are the essential principles and practices that guide nurses in delivering high-quality care to older adults, grounded in the best available scientific evidence, ensuring interventions are effective and tailored to the elderly population's unique needs.

    1Patient-centered care
    2Best available evidence
    3Interprofessional collaboration
    4Comprehensive geriatric assessment
    5Chronic disease management
    6Fall prevention & safety
    7Pain management
    8End-of-life & palliative care
    9Ethical & culturally competent care
    10Health promotion & prevention
    Memory aid — “People Expect Interprofessional Care; Chronic Falls Pain End Ethics Health”Patient-centered · Evidence · Interprofessional · CGA · Chronic disease · Falls · Pain · End-of-life · Ethics/culture · Health promotion.

    1. Patient-centered care & 2. Use of best available evidence PDF p. 13

    Patient-centered care — at the heart of evidence-based gerontological nursing — recognizes and respects the preferences, values and needs of older adults.

    • Individualized care plans — based on the patient's unique conditions, preferences and goals (chronic illness, mental health, functional independence).
    • Shared decision-making — engage older adults and families in informed decisions so care aligns with the patient's values and desired outcomes.

    Best available evidence — evidence-based practice is the integration (combination) of the best available scientific evidence from research into clinical decision-making.

    • Clinical guidelines — e.g., the American Geriatrics Society or the National Institute on Aging.
    • Systematic reviews and meta-analyses — high-level evidence for fall prevention, medication management, dementia care. (The module notes these may be considered as one level.)
    • Critical appraisal — the ability to assess the value or quality of studies for validity, reliability and applicability.
    Supplementary — where systematic reviews sitIn common evidence hierarchies, systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials sit at the top, followed by individual RCTs, cohort and case-control studies, descriptive studies, and expert opinion at the bottom. Hierarchies differ slightly between authors.

    3. Interprofessional collaboration PDF pp. 13–14

    Older adults' complex needs require many professionals; collaboration keeps care coordinated and comprehensive.

    • Multidisciplinary teams — doctors, pharmacists, physical therapists, social workers and others develop comprehensive care plans.
    • Care coordination — the nurse coordinates across settings (hospital, home care, long-term care) to ensure continuity and avoid fragmentation.
    • Communication — clear team communication prevents errors, reduces redundancies, improves outcomes.

    4. Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) PDF p. 14

    The CGA is a multidimensional and interdisciplinary process designed to evaluate an older adult's medical, psychological and functional status — crucial for early problem identification and a holistic care plan.

    DomainWhat is assessed
    Physical healthGeriatric syndromes — frailty, falls, incontinence, polypharmacy
    Functional statusADLs and IADLs → level of independence
    Cognitive & mental healthCognitive impairment, dementia, depression, delirium
    Social support systemsLiving situation, family support, social networks
    Supplementary — ADL vs IADL examplesADLs (basic self-care, e.g., Katz Index): bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, feeding. IADLs (independent living, e.g., Lawton scale): using the telephone, shopping, food preparation, housekeeping, laundry, transportation, managing medications, managing finances.

    5. Chronic disease management PDF p. 14

    Chronic illnesses — heart disease, diabetes, arthritis — are prevalent in older adults.

    • Self-management support — empower patients through education, monitoring and lifestyle change: medication adherence, symptom tracking, disease-specific strategies.
    • Multimorbidity management — address multiple conditions simultaneously, considering complex interactions between diseases and treatments.
    • Medication management — multiple drugs raise polypharmacy and adverse-reaction risk; regularly review for efficacy, safety and possible deprescribing.
    Clinical pearlDeprescribing means planned, supervised reduction or stopping of medicines that no longer benefit the patient or cause harm. The nurse identifies candidates and reports to the prescriber — the nurse does not stop prescribed drugs independently. Explanation

    6. Fall prevention & safety PDF p. 14

    Falls are a leading cause of injury among older adults.

    • Risk assessment — regular assessments with the Morse Fall Scale or Hendrich II Fall Risk Model (assigned by your instructor). Identify mobility issues, balance deficits, medications, environmental hazards.
    • Intervention strategies — strength and balance exercises, home modifications (removing tripping hazards), assistive devices.
    • Patient education — proper footwear, regular exercise, adequate nutrition.
    Supplementary: Morse Fall Scale (Morse, 1989)

    7. Pain management PDF p. 15

    Chronic pain is common from arthritis, neuropathy (nerve disease — stabbing, burning, tingling pain) or osteoporosis.

    • Pain assessment — choose the tool by cognitive status: Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) for those who can self-report; Pain Assessment in Advanced Dementia (PAINAD) for those who cannot (assigned by your instructor).
    • Multimodal pain management — combine pharmacological and nonpharmacological methods: analgesics, physical therapy, cognitive-behavioral therapy, massage, acupuncture.
    • Safe use of medications — know the risks of opioids (e.g., morphine) and other drugs: side effects, addiction, drug interactions.
    Supplementary: PAINAD scale (Warden, Hurley & Volicer, 2003)

    Exam trapOlder adults with dementia are often under-treated for pain because they cannot report it. Agitation, moaning or striking out may be pain behavior — assess with PAINAD before assuming a “behavioral problem.”

    8. End-of-life & palliative · 9. Ethical & culturally competent · 10. Health promotion PDF pp. 15–16

    8. End-of-life and palliative care — improve quality of life and manage symptoms

    • Symptom management — pain, dyspnea, fatigue, nausea.
    • Advance care planning — advance directives, living wills, DNR orders; informed decisions about care preferences.
    • Emotional and spiritual support — for patients and families.

    9. Ethical and culturally competent care — sensitivity to ethical and cultural needs

    • Cultural competence — respect differences in health beliefs, family dynamics, end-of-life preferences; tailor care to background and values.
    • Ethical decision-making — autonomy, informed consent, life-sustaining treatment decisions, with a patient-centered, evidence-based approach.

    10. Health promotion and prevention

    • Health screenings — hypertension, osteoporosis, cancer, cognitive impairment.
    • Vaccinations — e.g., influenza, pneumonia, shingles.
    • Lifestyle interventions — regular physical activity, proper nutrition, smoking cessation, social engagement.
    Key takeaways
    • EBP = best research evidence + clinical decision-making, tailored to the older person.
    • Tools by purpose: cognition MMSE/MoCA · falls Morse/Hendrich II · pain NRS (can self-report) / PAINAD (advanced dementia) · spiritual FICA.
    • The CGA covers physical, functional, cognitive/mental and social domains.

    Unit 5 · Topic 5 · Module p. 16

    Laws Affecting Senior Citizens / Older Persons

    The Philippine laws that grant benefits and privileges to Filipinos aged 60 and above — and what each means for nursing practice.

    1. Republic Act 7432 — Senior Citizens Act of 1992 PDF p. 16

    Grants benefits and privileges to Filipino citizens aged 60 years and above.

    Key provisions (as listed in the module)

    • 20% discount on medicines, transportation and basic services.
    • Exemption from Value-Added Tax (VAT) on certain goods and services.
    • Priority in express lanes and public facilities.
    • Tax incentives to establishments providing discounts.

    Nursing implication: ensure senior citizens are aware of and can avail of their entitled benefits, and advocate for their rights in health institutions.

    2. Republic Act 9257 — Expanded Senior Citizens Act of 2003 PDF p. 16

    Strengthened RA 7432 by expanding privileges and providing more social support and healthcare access.

    Added benefits

    • Free medical and dental services in government facilities.
    • Continuing employment opportunities for those physically fit to work.
    • Educational assistance for older adults and their dependents.

    Nursing implication: promote inclusion of older adults in health programs; encourage active participation in community activities.

    3. Republic Act 9994 — Expanded Senior Citizens Act of 2010 PDF p. 16

    Further improved health and social welfare programs for the elderly.

    Key features (as listed in the module)

    • Mandatory PhilHealth coverage for senior citizens.
    • Social pension for indigent senior citizens.
    • Access to home care and community-based rehabilitation.

    Nursing implication: collaborate with local government units (LGUs) and social workers so seniors receive comprehensive care and assistance.

    RA 9257Expanded Senior Citizens Act, 2003Free medical/dental in government facilities; continuing employment; educational assistanceInclusion; community participation

    Memory aid — “92 · 03 · 10”7432 started it (1992) → 9257 expanded it (2003: free care, work, school) → 9994 expanded again (2010: PhilHealth, pension, home care). The nursing implications climb from inform → include → collaborate.
    Supplementary — legal updates and clarifications (verify for your exam)
    • VAT exemption: the explicit VAT exemption for seniors' covered purchases was introduced by RA 9994 (2010); before this, the 12% VAT effectively reduced the value of the 20% discount. RA 9994 now provides a 20% discount and VAT exemption on covered goods and services (medicines, medical and dental services, transport fares, restaurants, funeral services, etc.).
    • PhilHealth: RA 9994 provided PhilHealth coverage for indigent seniors. RA 10645 (2014) made PhilHealth coverage mandatory for all senior citizens regardless of economic status.
    • Follow the module's lists for your midterm unless your instructor says otherwise; these notes explain how the laws currently read.

    Unit 5 · Topic 6 · Module pp. 16–17

    Polypharmacy & Ethical Principles in Gerontologic Nursing

    The medication risks unique to older adults, and the seven ethical principles that guide every nursing decision.

    B. Medications of older adults (polypharmacy) PDF pp. 16–17

    Polypharmacy is the use of multiple medications (usually ≥5) by a patient — common among older adults with multiple chronic conditions.

    Risks

    • Increased adverse drug reactions
    • Drug–drug interactions
    • Non-adherence due to complexity of the regimen
    • Cognitive impairment leading to medication errors

    Nursing responsibilities

    • Conduct a comprehensive medication review
    • Educate about proper adherence
    • Monitor for side effects and toxicities
    • Collaborate with physicians and pharmacists to simplify regimens
    Deeper dive: why older adults are more vulnerable

    With aging, kidney and liver clearance decline and body fat increases relative to water, so many drugs stay longer and reach higher levels. Several prescribers may each add drugs without seeing the whole list, and a side effect may be mistaken for a new illness and treated with yet another drug (a “prescribing cascade”). Explanation

    Supplementary — the AGS Beers Criteria®The American Geriatrics Society publishes the Beers Criteria (most recent update 2023), a list of potentially inappropriate medications for older adults — for example, strongly anticholinergic drugs and benzodiazepines, which can worsen confusion and increase falls. Nurses use it to flag medications for prescriber review.
    Clinical pearl — a practical medication reviewAsk the patient to bring all medicines (including over-the-counter, herbal and vitamins) — the “brown bag” review — and compare them with the prescribed list, checking duplicates, interactions, timing and whether the patient can explain each one. Explanation

    C. Ethical principles in gerontologic nursing PDF p. 17

    #PrincipleMeaning (module)Bedside example

    2BeneficenceAct in the best interest of the patient to promote well-being.Arranging physical therapy to restore mobility.

    Unit 5 · Topic 7 · Module pp. 17–18

    Long-Term Care, Palliative Care, Advance Directives & End-of-Life Care

    The settings and philosophies of continuing care, and the legal documents that protect an older person's wishes.

    D. Long-term care PDF p. 17

    Long-term care involves ongoing health and personal care services for individuals unable to perform daily activities independently.

    Settings: nursing homes, assisted living facilities, home-based care.

    Goals

    • Maintain the highest possible quality of life
    • Promote independence and dignity
    • Prevent complications and institutionalization

    Nursing role

    • Create individualized care plans
    • Provide rehabilitative and preventive interventions
    • Support family caregivers

    E. Palliative care PDF p. 17

    Focuses on relieving suffering and improving quality of life for patients with life-limiting illnesses. It is provided alongside curative treatment or as comfort-focused care.

    Core aspects

    • Pain and symptom management
    • Emotional, psychosocial and spiritual support
    • Family involvement in decision-making

    Nursing role: assess, comfort, communicate empathetically, and advocate for the patient's wishes.

    Exam trapPalliative care is not only for the dying and does not require stopping curative treatment. End-of-life care is the part of the continuum when the focus is comfort and dignity rather than cure.

    F. Advance directives / DNR (Do Not Resuscitate) PDF p. 18

    Advance directives — legal documents that express a person's wishes regarding medical treatment if they become incapacitated. Examples: Living Will, Durable Power of Attorney for Healthcare.

    DNR order — a physician's order not to initiate CPR in the event of cardiac or respiratory arrest.

    DocumentWhat it doesWho decides
    Living willStates in writing which treatments the person wants or refuses if unable to decide.The patient, in advance
    Durable power of attorney for healthcareNames a trusted person (proxy) to make healthcare decisions when the patient cannot.The appointed proxy, guided by the patient's wishes
    DNR orderMedical order: no CPR at arrest. Other appropriate treatment and comfort care continue.Written by the physician, consistent with patient/proxy wishes

    Descriptions in the “What it does” column are standard definitions added for clarity; the module lists the examples.

    Nursing responsibilities

    • Ensure clarity and documentation of the patient's wishes.
    • Respect decisions and avoid personal bias.
    • Provide emotional support to the family.
    • Educate patients on their rights to informed choice.
    Clinical pearl“DNR” does not mean “do not treat.” Patients with a DNR order still receive medications, nutrition, pain relief and nursing care. Explanation

    G. End-of-life care PDF p. 18

    Care for patients in the final stage of life, focusing on comfort and dignity rather than cure.

    Components

    • Pain control, emotional support, spiritual care
    • Family communication and involvement in decisions
    • Respect for cultural and religious values

    Nursing implications

    • Manage physical symptoms (dyspnea, pain, anorexia)
    • Provide presence, listening and reassurance
    • Help the family prepare for the dying process

    WhoCannot perform daily activities independentlyLife-limiting illness, any stageFinal stage of life
    FocusQuality of life, independence, dignityRelief of suffering, quality of lifeComfort and dignity, not cure

    Unit 5 · Topic 8 · Module pp. 18–19

    Care of the Dying & the Dead, Spirituality, and Ethical Dilemmas

    Dignified care at the end of life and after death, meeting spiritual needs, and reasoning through situations with no clear “right” answer.

    H. Care of the dying and the dead PDF p. 18

    Care of the dying

    • Maintain comfort, cleanliness and dignity
    • Provide a peaceful environment and privacy
    • Encourage family presence and expression of feelings

    Postmortem care

    1. Verify death as declared by a physician
    2. Respectful body care — closing eyes, mouth, positioning
    3. Allow the family to view and say goodbye
    4. Document; handle belongings per hospital policy
    5. Follow infection control and cultural practices
    Deeper dive: rationale for the postmortem sequence

    Death must be pronounced by the physician before postmortem care begins. Positioning the body supine with the head slightly elevated and closing the eyes and mouth soon after death helps preserve a natural appearance before rigor mortis sets in and prevents blood pooling in the face. Belongings are listed and released per policy to protect the family and the institution; standard precautions continue because body fluids may still be infectious. Explanation

    I. Spirituality among older persons PDF pp. 18–19

    Spirituality provides meaning, hope and comfort, especially during illness or at the end of life.

    Common spiritual needs

    • Reconciliation and forgiveness
    • Peace, hope, connection with God or a higher power
    • Sense of purpose and legacy

    Nursing role

    • Conduct spiritual assessment (e.g., FICA tool)
    • Support faith-based practices
    • Collaborate with chaplains or spiritual counselors
    • Be nonjudgmental and culturally sensitive
    Supplementary — the FICA spiritual history (Puchalski & Romer, 2000)
    • F — Faith, belief, meaning: “Do you consider yourself spiritual or religious? What gives your life meaning?”
    • I — Importance and influence: “How important is your faith? How does it influence your health decisions?”
    • C — Community: “Are you part of a spiritual or religious community?”
    • A — Address in care: “How would you like us to address these issues in your care?”

    J. Ethical dilemmas in gerontologic nursing PDF p. 19

    Ethical dilemmas arise when values conflict and no clear “right” answer exists.

    Common dilemma (module)Principles in tension Analysis
    1. Use of life-sustaining treatments (e.g., ventilator for terminally ill patients)Beneficence vs nonmaleficence; autonomy
    2. Withholding or withdrawing nutrition and hydrationBeneficence vs nonmaleficence; autonomy; cultural/religious values
    3. Decisions about resuscitation and DNR ordersAutonomy vs family wishes; beneficence
    4. Allocation of limited resources (e.g., ICU beds)Justice
    5. Elder abuse and neglect reporting vs confidentialityConfidentiality vs nonmaleficence/beneficence (safety)
    6. Autonomy vs safety (e.g., a cognitively impaired elder refuses care)Autonomy vs beneficence/nonmaleficence

    Nursing responsibilities

    • Apply ethical reasoning and critical thinking.
    • Engage in interdisciplinary ethics consultations.
    • Advocate for the patient's rights, dignity and preferences.
    • Document all decisions and communications clearly.
    Clinical pearl — when an elder discloses abuseConfidentiality is not absolute when a person is at risk of harm. The nurse ensures immediate safety, documents objectively, and follows institutional and legal reporting procedures with the team (e.g., social worker) — explaining this honestly to the patient (veracity). Explanation
    Key takeaways
    • Postmortem care begins only after the physician declares death.
    • Spiritual care = assessment (FICA), support of practices, chaplain referral, nonjudgmental presence.
    • In dilemmas, the nurse does not decide alone: reason ethically, consult the team, advocate, document.

    Priority nursing interventions

    • Vision: adequate lighting, reduce glare, contrasting colors, clear pathways, eye exams, eye-drop adherence.
    • Hearing: face the patient, speak clearly and slowly, don't shout, reduce noise, working hearing aid, check cerumen.
    • Touch: daily skin/foot checks, safe water temperature, proper footwear, mobility aids, fall prevention.
    • Smell/taste: smoke and gas safety, check food freshness, herbs and spices not salt, oral hygiene, monitor nutrition.
    • Chronic confusion: MMSE/MoCA, reorientation (early) or validation (late), routine, one instruction at a time, wander prevention, review meds, respite care, advance directives early.
    • Communication: simple language, picture boards/AAC, allow time, face patient, SLP referral, teach family, watch for nonverbal distress.
    • Polypharmacy: medication review, adherence teaching, monitor toxicity, simplify regimen with physician and pharmacist.
    • End of life: manage pain/dyspnea/anorexia, presence, prepare family; postmortem care only after physician declares death.

    Common exam traps

    1. “Shout so the patient can hear” — never; speak clearly at moderate pace.
    2. “Add salt to improve flavor” — no; use herbs and spices.
    3. “Correct the advanced-dementia patient's false belief” — no; validate feelings, redirect.
    4. “Sudden confusion is just dementia progression” — no; suspect delirium, report.
    5. “Broca's patient cannot understand” — wrong; Broca's understands, Wernicke's doesn't.
    6. “DNR means stop all treatment” — wrong; it means no CPR.
    7. “Palliative care only when cure is stopped” — wrong; can be alongside curative care.
    8. “Agitated dementia patient → sedate” — first assess for pain (PAINAD) and other unmet needs.
    9. “Begin postmortem care when the nurse finds no pulse” — wrong; death must be declared by the physician.
    10. “The nurse resolves ethical dilemmas alone” — wrong; consult interdisciplinary ethics team and document.
